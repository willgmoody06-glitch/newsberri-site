STEP 1 · THE SOURCES

It starts with the sources

NewsBerri listens to live news feeds around the clock — 24 hours a day, five days a week. The moment a headline crosses any of them, it shows up on your tape marked provisional. There's no queue and no hold while the AI thinks; you can read the story before we have.

Provisional isn't an apology — it's just an honest label. It means “we see it, we haven't read it yet.” The row already carries its timestamp and its source, with a small PROV chip sitting exactly where the score is about to land. Most headlines wear the label for well under a minute.

Why several sources instead of one big one? Mostly coverage — stories break in different places. But there's a second reason that pays off in step 3: you can't confirm a story with only one source, and confirmation is a big part of how the read earns your trust.

STEP 2 · THE SCORING STEP

An AI actually reads the story

Next, an LLM reads the story — the whole story, not a keyword match — and scores it for each contract it touches. Three judgments, every time: direction, on a −100 to +100 sentiment scale; impact, how much this kind of story tends to matter; and confidence, how sure the read is. The same headline can be bad news for the Nasdaq and fine news for gold, so every contract gets its own line.

Each score is made once, on our servers, and then sent to everyone at the same time. It's not recomputed per person, not tilted toward your positions, not softened to keep you comfortable. Everyone looking at NQ sees the same read at the same moment — which is exactly what makes it worth looking at.

And because a number is a summary, not an argument, the reasoning stays attached. Tap ✦ Explain on any scored story and you get the why, in plain language: what the story says, why it scored the way it did, and what that means for the contract you're looking at.

STEP 3 · A SECOND SOURCE

A second source makes it solid

One source carrying a story is a report. Two sources carrying it is about as close to a fact as a fast tape gets. So when a second source confirms a headline, we don't print it again — the row you already have firms up in place, and its label changes to corroborated.

That means every headline on the tape sits at one of three stages, and the label always tells you which: provisional (“it just crossed — we see it, we haven't read it yet”), scored (“we've read it, and here's what we think it means”), and corroborated (“a second source agrees it actually happened”). No row ever pretends to more certainty than it has.

And when a story never finds a second source? It stays visibly single-sourced, and its confidence stays modest. That's the tape's polite way of saying “maybe.”

STEP 4 · ONE NUMBER PER CONTRACT

Everything rolls up into one number

Individual stories come and go; the index is the running mood. For every contract, the recent scored stories roll up into a single number, weighted by freshness — a story from five minutes ago counts a lot, one from fifty minutes ago barely registers. The result answers a simple question: what does the news look like for this contract, right now?

That happens for 42 contracts across 7 asset classes — equity index, rates, energy, metals, agriculture, FX and crypto — with sector and asset-class rollups above them, and every plan gets all of it. When a contract's number turns genuinely decisive, it gets a plain-words label like STRONG BEAR — and from that moment the call sits on the public track record (step 7), where it'll be resolved against whatever actually happens next.

One thing worth repeating, because it governs everything here: every number on this page is sentiment, on a −100 to +100 scale. None of it is ever a market price, and none of it ever tells you what to do.

STEP 5 · THE CALENDAR & GUARDRAIL

The calendar sees releases coming

Everything so far reacts to news that already happened. The calendar works ahead of it. Every scheduled release that touches your contracts is on it — CPI, auctions, jobless claims — and for each one, the what-ifs are written in advance: what a stronger-than-expected number would likely mean for your contract, what a weaker one would. When the release lands, the meaning is already waiting for it.

Then the Guardrail gives you a heads-up three times on the way in. ARMED means the release is 30 minutes out — the terminal is already watching it for you. HOT at ten means it's getting close. LIVE at two means it's about to happen. Each step brings a message on screen, a notification, and a spoken callout, so a release shouldn't arrive unannounced no matter where you're looking. And if your prop firm has rules about trading through releases, Topstep's and Apex's are on screen next to the countdown, so following them on time is straightforward.

The chip carries one small piece of history too, always in the same plain format — HIST ±0.1% TYP 15M (N=15): how this release has typically behaved, for how long, across how many past occurrences. It's the only backward-looking number we allow ourselves, and it's there so you can decide how much to care before the countdown runs out.

STEP 6 · THE SQUAWK

The squawk says it out loud

Trading floors have kept a squawk box on for decades — a voice reading the news out loud so nobody has to stare at the tape. NewsBerri builds that voice into the terminal: headlines above your chosen importance level are read aloud as they land and as they firm up, in one calm sentence that includes the read — what happened, and what it means for the contract.

The bar is yours to set. Keep it high and the squawk only speaks when something genuinely matters; open it up and it quietly narrates the working day. Either way, you'll hear the headline even when you're not looking — eyes on your platform, ears on the news.

The Guardrail borrows the same voice for its two-minute callout. Between the two of them, nothing on the calendar or the tape should land on you cold.

STEP 7 · THE TRACK RECORD

We keep score in public

A tool that grades its own homework in private isn't worth much, so we don't. Whenever the read turns decisive enough to earn a label, that call is logged publicly the moment it's made — contract, label, timestamp — and later resolved against what actually happened next.

Misses stay on the record next to the hits. Nothing gets quietly deleted, reworded, or resolved generously after the fact. And the record stays qualitative on purpose — we don't compute an accuracy percentage about ourselves, because a number you calculate about yourself is really just an advertisement. Read the ledger and make up your own mind; that's the whole idea.

And the ledger is public from the first call — nothing curated, nothing held back. Open it whenever you like and check the calls against what actually happened. It's a record you can verify for yourself, not a number you have to trust.

STEP 8 · THE FINE PRINT, UP FRONT

What we deliberately don't do

Two lists that matter as much as anything above: the things we've chosen to leave out on purpose, and what happens on the bad days.

You won't find here

  • No market prices, no charts, no candlesticks. Your trading platform already does price, beautifully. We sit beside it and handle the meaning — we never repeat its job.
  • No buy or sell calls. We tell you what a story means for a contract. What you do about it is entirely, permanently yours.
  • No promised outcomes. The Guardrail gives you a heads-up before every scheduled release and puts your firm's rules on screen. It helps you decide on time — that's the honest limit of what any tool can do.
  • No manufactured applause. No invented testimonials, no decorative percentages. The public track record above is the only scoreboard we keep.
LEFT OUT ON PURPOSE — NOT BY OVERSIGHT

If something breaks

  • Headlines keep flowing. If one source stalls, the others keep going. If the scoring itself has a bad moment, stories still arrive — marked provisional, exactly like step 1.
  • You'll see the problem honestly. A stretch with more PROV chips than usual is the system telling you the truth about what it knows, not hiding a fault.
  • Nothing is back-filled. When scoring catches up, labels advance in place — provisional to scored to corroborated. They're never quietly rewritten to look like we knew all along.
